04 · AI & Machine Learning

Models that reach production, on data you can defend.

Machine learning projects rarely fail at the modelling step. They fail because features were computed differently in training than in serving, because nobody noticed the input distribution moved, or because the underlying data was never trustworthy enough to build on. We work the whole chain, starting with the part underneath.

Discuss a use case → Estimate an AI programme

Where we start

Almost every engagement begins with a readiness question rather than a modelling question, because that is where the risk sits.

Is the data there?

Do the features you need exist, historically, at the grain and frequency the model requires? Missing history is the most common reason a promising use case stalls.

Is it trustworthy?

Consistent definitions, known lineage, acceptable quality. A model amplifies whatever inconsistency exists in its inputs and makes it harder to see.

Is the decision real?

Is there a specific decision someone will make differently because of the prediction? If not, the project produces a dashboard nobody uses and an ongoing maintenance cost.

Services

Feature pipelines & feature stores

Reproducible feature engineering with a single definition shared between training and serving, so the model sees the same thing in production that it saw in training. Backfill strategy, point-in-time correctness and versioning included.

Model training workflows

Versioned, reproducible training with experiment tracking, a model registry and a documented record of what shipped, when, on which data and why. Forecasting, classification, anomaly detection, risk scoring and segmentation.

MLOps & deployment

Deployment pipelines with staged rollout and rollback paths, batch and real-time serving patterns, and infrastructure defined as code alongside the rest of your estate.

Monitoring & retraining

Drift detection on inputs and outputs, performance monitoring against ground truth as it arrives, alerting that reaches a human, and retraining schedules with clear promotion criteria.

Retrieval-augmented generation

Document ingestion, chunking, embedding and retrieval over your own corpus. Evaluation harnesses, citation of source passages, and honest measurement of answer quality rather than demo-driven enthusiasm.

LLM application & workflow design

Structured extraction from unstructured documents, classification and routing, and agentic workflows with human checkpoints where the cost of an error justifies one.

AI governance & model risk

Model documentation, lineage from training data through to prediction, bias and fairness assessment, approval records, and the evidence pack a regulator or internal model risk function will eventually request.

Responsible AI assessment

Structured review of a proposed use case against harm, explainability, human oversight and data protection considerations — including the EU AI Act risk classification where it applies.

How an engagement runs

  1. Week 1

    Readiness and framing

    Confirm the decision the model supports, audit feature availability and quality, and agree the success metric before any modelling starts.

  2. Weeks 2–4

    Feature pipeline and baseline

    Build the feature pipeline first, then a deliberately simple baseline model. If the baseline is good enough, we say so rather than adding complexity for its own sake.

  3. Weeks 4–8

    Model development and evaluation

    Iterate against a held-out set with honest evaluation, including where the model performs badly and for whom.

  4. Weeks 8–12

    Deployment, monitoring and handover

    Ship behind a rollback path, wire up drift and performance monitoring, write the runbooks, and hand over to your team with documentation they can act on.

Scope boundaries

What we do not do. We do not train foundation models, and we do not take on research-grade problems requiring novel architectures. We build on hosted models and established techniques, applied carefully to your data. If your problem needs a research team, we will say so.

Common questions

Do we need a proper data platform before we do machine learning?
Usually yes, at least for the slice of data the model depends on. Models need consistent definitions, known lineage and reliable delivery. Where that layer is not ready we propose fixing it first — often as a small, separately-priced engagement — because a model trained on unreliable inputs will fail in production no matter how careful the modelling was.
Can you work with our existing data science team?
That is the most common arrangement. Many teams have capable data scientists and no path to production. We build the pipeline, deployment and monitoring layer around their models and hand it over, rather than replacing work they have already done.
Do you build large language model applications?
Yes, principally retrieval-augmented generation over your own documents, structured extraction from unstructured sources, and workflow automation with human checkpoints. We build on hosted foundation models. We do not train our own and would be sceptical of anyone your size who suggests you should.
How do you evaluate whether a model is actually good?
Against the decision it supports, not against a leaderboard metric. That means agreeing the success measure with you before development starts, evaluating on held-out data that reflects production conditions, and reporting honestly where the model performs badly and for which segments.
Who owns the models and the code?
You do, from the first commit. Code in your repository, models in your registry, infrastructure defined as code you control. No proprietary framework to license afterwards.
What does an AI engagement typically cost?

A readiness assessment typically falls between $6,000 and $8,400, and a production deployment engagement starts from around $15,000, though both depend heavily on the state of your data.
